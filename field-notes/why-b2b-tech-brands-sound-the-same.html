Why so many B2B tech brands sound the same

And how to fix it, with a trick borrowed from screenwriters.

Field note no. 1 · Written by Kevin Logist
17 November 2026

FADE IN:

INT. Five browser tabs – any weekday

Screenwriters have an old trick for testing dialogue. Cover the character names on the page and read the lines back. If you can still tell who’s talking, the characters are alive. If you can’t, you’ve written one person in five costumes.

Now try it on B2B tech. Open the homepages of five companies in the same category, put your thumb over the logos and read the first line out loud.

  • A) Empowering businesses with innovative solutions.
  • B) Unlock the full potential of your data.
  • C) The all-in-one platform for modern teams.
  • D) Seamless. Scalable. Secure.
  • E) Accelerate growth with AI-powered insights.

Kevin

(to us)

Who’s who? No idea. I’m not sure the companies know either.

Those five are composites, before anyone starts checking. Any resemblance to your own homepage is, sadly, not a coincidence.

Belgium has three official languages. B2B tech has quietly added a fourth, and nobody speaks it at home. I’ve written my share of it too, and I was proud of those lines for at least an afternoon.

So why does a sector full of clever products end up sounding like one long press release? It isn’t a lack of talent. It’s four forces, and each of them is perfectly reasonable on its own. That’s exactly what makes them so hard to beat.

Flashback – a computer department, 1978

Force one: fear. “No one ever got fired for buying IBM.” A computer trade magazine already called that line conventional wisdom in 1978, and it still explains half of B2B marketing. A business buyer isn’t only choosing a product. They’re putting their name on a decision that their boss, their finance director and their IT team will remember for years.

So brands dress for safety: a blue logo, a handshake, a dashboard floating at a jaunty angle and the word “trusted” somewhere near the top. It all looks very reassuring. The trouble is that when everyone looks safe, looking safe stops telling the buyer anything.

INT. A meeting room – Friday, 17:40

Force two: the committee. A homepage line goes into the review round with a sharp edge. Product sands off one corner, sales another, legal a third, and then someone asks whether we can actually prove “best”. Every reviewer removes one risk. What survives is the sentence nobody objects to, which is also the sentence nobody remembers.

Nobody in that room is wrong, by the way. That’s what makes it tricky.

INT. The same meeting room – next quarter

Force three: copying the category. We benchmark. Of course we do. We study the market leader and our three closest competitors, take notes and make sure we’re “in line with the market”. Do that across a whole sector for a few years and you get convergent evolution: everyone ends up shaped like the market leader, minus the head start.

INT. A chat window – now

Force four, and the newest: the machine that averages. Ask an AI model for a tagline for your software company and watch what comes back. Empowering. Unlock. Seamless. It isn’t broken. It’s doing exactly what it was built to do, which is predicting the most likely next word. And in B2B tech, the most likely next word is the one everybody already uses.

There’s research on this. In a 2024 study in Science Advances, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser had 300 people write very short stories, some with ideas from an AI model and some without. The stories written with AI help were rated as more creative and better written, especially when the writer wasn’t very creative to begin with. They were also more similar to each other. Better one by one, more alike as a group. If that isn’t a description of B2B tech marketing, I don’t know what is.

I build things with AI in my spare time, so I’m no sceptic. But use it as a ghostwriter and it will hand you the category average, beautifully formatted. Turn it around, though, and it’s a brilliant cliché detector. Ask it for the ten most predictable taglines in your category, and you’ve got a list of ten lines you’re no longer allowed to write.

INT. A buyer’s memory – months before the deal

Does any of this matter, as long as the product is good? It does, because most of your buyers aren’t buying. Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, working with LinkedIn’s B2B Institute, put it in a simple rule: at any given moment, only about 5% of B2B buyers are in the market. The other 95% will buy one day, just not this quarter.

So the real job of a B2B brand is to be remembered by those 95% when their day comes. And it’s very hard to remember a company you couldn’t tell apart in the first place.

In tech there’s a second audience reading the same homepage: the engineers and developers you’d love to have on your team. They tend to read it with one eyebrow raised.

INT. The writers’ room – daynight

So how do you fix it? Here are five moves that have nothing to do with a bigger budget. Most of them need more nerve than money.

  1. Cover the names, on yourself. Put your homepage line next to those of your four closest competitors, take the logos off and show the page to someone who doesn’t work for you. If they can’t pick you out, you don’t have a copy problem. You have a positioning problem dressed up as one.

  2. Finish the sentence. “We are the only … that …”. Marty Neumeier calls this the onliness test, in his book Zag. If you can’t finish it without “innovative” or “end-to-end”, no copywriter can save you. If you can, the copy almost writes itself.

  3. Sell the ride, not the saddle. On 31 July 2013, two weeks before Slack’s preview release, Stewart Butterfield sent his team a memo called “We Don’t Sell Saddles Here”. His company had shut down its online game, Glitch, at the end of 2012 and was betting everything on the chat tool it had built along the way. His point: a saddle maker can compete on the quality of its leather, or it can sell horseback riding. Slack, he wrote, was selling “a reduction in information overload, relief from stress”. Not features. Relief.

    As someone who builds games at the weekend, I find it deeply comforting that one of the best B2B brand memos ever written came out of a game that flopped.

  4. Pick a fight with a problem. In February 2000, a young company called Salesforce sent protesters, who were in fact paid actors, to a big rival’s user conference, carrying “No Software” signs. Its logo was the word “software” in a red circle with a line through it. Technically that was a fight with a competitor, but the enemy on the signs was an idea: software you had to install, maintain and upgrade yourself.

    I’m not recommending hiring actors. I am recommending having something to protest. An enemy gives a brand an opinion, and an opinion is the quickest way to stop sounding like everyone else. (You may have noticed on my homepage that I have a soft spot for crossing things out.)

  5. Write it the way your best salesperson says it over coffee. Every B2B company has someone who can explain the product in two sentences at the coffee machine and get a nod. That’s your brand voice. It just hasn’t been written down yet.

    For six and a half years I ran Ondernemers, a magazine for entrepreneurs, and the founders I interviewed were nearly always better at explaining their company across a table than their own brochure was.

    Some companies do write it down. Mailchimp’s public style guide describes its voice as plainspoken, genuine and dry-humoured, and, my favourite, as translators, whose job is “to demystify B2B-speak”. Then read every line out loud. If you wouldn’t say it to one person across a table, don’t publish it to thousands.

And once you’ve found the sentence, protect it. That’s the hardest part. Let the reviewers check the facts, not sand down the adjectives, and let one person hold the pen. That isn’t vanity. It’s how anything with a voice gets made, from a screenplay to a homepage.

EXT. Off Martha’s Vineyard – summer, 1974

A young director is shooting a film about a shark, and the mechanical shark keeps breaking down in the salt water. So Steven Spielberg has to suggest the shark instead of showing it: a fin, a few yellow barrels, two notes of music. Almost fifty years later he told the BBC that Jaws was “a much better movie” because the shark kept breaking down.

Two notes, and you know exactly who’s coming. That’s the whole test, really.

Kevin

Cover the names. If you can still tell who’s talking, you have a brand. If you can’t, you have a category.
